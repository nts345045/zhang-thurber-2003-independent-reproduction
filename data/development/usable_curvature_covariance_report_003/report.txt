     An independent double-difference tomography implementation:
     synthetic reconstruction and limits of manuscript reproduction
                               Independent computational reproduction

                                                October 2026


                                                    Abstract
         Double-difference tomography uses paired seismic arrivals to reduce shared travel-time errors
     while estimating source locations and velocity jointly. Here we implement this inverse problem in-
     dependently in C++ and test it against the written model of Zhang and Thurber (2003). The code
     combines variational ray tracing, trilinear velocity interpolation, joint linearization, and damped,
     smoothed least squares. We reconstruct the specified synthetic velocity profile and generate new
     event and station geometries. Consequently, the true-model panel is independently reproduced,
     whereas synthetic inversion panels are partial reproductions. The initial project instructions excluded
     Hayward observations. A later authorized acquisition supplies an independently selected, identified
     geometry for a partial Figure 4 counterpart. The revised solver also generates partial counterparts
     to the Hayward relocation, velocity, and conditional resolution panels from catalog observations. All
     numerical figures in this report use saved output from this implementation. The result is a repro-
     ducible method investigation, not a recovery of the published Hayward velocity model. The resumed
     work permits authoritative observational inputs. A later section records authoritative metadata in-
     vestigation and ray-kernel corrections. The synthetic inverse solutions retain their original baseline
     provenance.


1    Scientific scope and evidence
The target paper is Zhang and Thurber (2003), Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America 93,
1875–1889, doi:10.1785/0120020190. Its written equations, experiment descriptions, and captions are
the scientific specification. The publisher page returned HTTP 403; the complete manuscript transcrip-
tion posted by an author on ResearchGate provided the text. The baseline calculations used no original
model code, source observations, synthetic arrival files, plotting scripts, or figure files. No published
plot was digitized. No published image appears in this report.
    The initial exclusion of source observations had a direct scientific consequence. A physical forward
model can generate new arrival times for a specified geometry, but it cannot recover the historical
earthquake realization and picking errors from a prose description. The baseline therefore separated
a reconstructible velocity specification, an independent synthetic inverse experiment, and unavailable
field-data inversions. Later sections document the authorized authoritative-data retrieval and subse-
quent calculations. The panel audit preserves all eleven manuscript figures and records each remaining
experimental difference. No supplement is listed for this article on the original SSA issue contents or
its original abstract page. No supplementary figures were identified. The official issue contents marks
supplements for other articles explicitly; reference/specification_notes.md records those checks.

Velocity sensitivities from both events enter the difference, subject to the 5 m sparsification rule de-
scribed below. This includes changes in path anomalies with source location. Shared station timing
errors cancel in differenced observations, whereas independent pick errors do not. Catalog differences
share errors with each other and with absolute picks; our weighting follows the staged formulation
without whitening this covariance.
    The linear step minimizes a weighted residual norm with first-difference regularization of the final
log slowness and damping of the column-scaled increment. A first-difference row divides the difference
between adjacent nodes by their separation. Columns are normalized to unit Euclidean norm before
damping rows are added. An independently written LSQR implementation solves the sparse system
without forming normal equations. Absolute, catalog-difference, and precise-difference rows receive
stage weights (1, 0.1, 0.01), (0.1, 1, 0.01), and (0.001, 0.01, 1), respectively. Smoothing and damping
choices are explicit in the configuration. After a rejected update, identical repetitions within the same
stage are skipped, while every later weight stage is retained.
    Each accepted nonlinear step must decrease the current weighted misfit plus roughness. Weights
are frozen within that line search. Trial source locations are projected onto the grid domain; velocities
remain positive by logarithmic parameterization. No published solution is used to choose an update.
Sources at boundaries and failed updates are counted in the results because they can indicate model
error or weak constraints.

2.1    Ray integration and numerical substitutions
The baseline ray tracer minimizes discretized Fermat time with limited-memory BFGS and Armijo
backtracking, starting from a straight path. Each segment is split at grid-plane crossings and inte-
grated with two-point Gaussian quadrature. This independent variational method replaces the paper’s
pseudo-bending implementation. It searches for a local stationary path, with no guarantee of finding
every competing first-arrival branch. Search directions and convergence gradients respect active model
boundaries. Near-coincident interior nodes are merged at a numerical tolerance far below ray spacing,
with the quasi-Newton history reset after such topology changes. Independent rays are traced in parallel
with OpenMP.
    The reference configuration uses 1 km initial ray spacing and 180 ray iterations, 18 nonlinear it-
erations in three equal stages, smoothing weight 5, and scaled damping 0.1. These choices are not
all specified by the paper. Distance and residual biweights, a 5 m differential path-coefficient cutoff,
regularization scaling, line search, and the residual-scale estimator are fully defined in METHODS.md and

the source. A damping or smoothing number cannot be transferred between implementations without
its scaling convention. Our equal-direction gradient penalty should therefore not be assumed identical
to the original regularization.


3     Independent synthetic experiment
The true medium is constant with depth and along strike. Its cross-strike profile is 6 km/s for x ≤ 0,
falls linearly to 4 km/s at x = 1 km, remains 4 km/s through x = 5 km, rises linearly to 5 km/s
at x = 6 km, and increases linearly to 6 km/s at x = 38 km. The paper describes sharp gradients
over x = 0–1 and 5–6 km; linear interpolation across these two transitions is our explicit assumption.
Figure 1 is generated from saved model sampling and reconstructs the written velocity specification
with this assumption, without using published pixel values.
    The inversion grid follows the coordinates in manuscript caption 2: x = (−35, −15, 0, 2, 4, 6, 20, 35)
km, y = (−60, −40, −20, 0, 20, 40) km, and z = (0, 3, 7, 11, 16) km. The forward grid adds the profile
breakpoints x = 1 and 5 km. This prevents the data generator from using exactly the same coarse
representation as the inverse problem.
    We generate 120 sources and 32 surface stations independently with seed 2003. Half the sources
sample a 12 km wide region around the trough, and half sample a broader 40 km interval; all sample
the model volume in depth and along strike. Stations use a jittered surface array. Figure 2 shows
the complete generated geometry. These are not the paper’s Loma Prieta observations. The starting
velocity is homogeneous at 5.5 km/s, substituting for the paper’s referenced layered starting model,
whose values are not tabulated in the target article. Independent starting source-coordinate errors have
standard deviation 0.6 km, with an initial depth restriction of 0.5–15.5 km. Starting origin-time errors
have standard deviation 0.05 s.
    We round forward travel times to 0.01 s, add independent 0.04 s Gaussian pick noise and a station-
constant uniform bias between −0.3 and 0.3 s. Catalog differences subtract these noisy picks. Precise
synthetic differences subtract rounded forward times before added pick noise. This explicitly resolves
an ambiguity in the text’s noise ordering in favor of precise pair differences. Every event is observed
at every station, and pairs within 20 km are selected. The saved arrival table separates exact time,
rounding, station bias, pick noise, and final observed time. Subtracting two separately rounded arrivals
permits differential rounding errors approaching 0.01 s. The paper quotes 0.005 s for its differential
data; this discrepancy remains a limitation of our explicit rounding order.

3.1   Starting-model sensitivity
The target paper does not identify a side of the Dietz–Ellsworth model. USGS Open-File Report 93-
578, Table 4, tabulates two gradient adaptations attributed to that work. We sample each named
profile (LOM and LON) onto the same inversion-depth grid and repeat DD inversion using otherwise
identical inputs. The adaptation footnote prevents identifying either as the confirmed target start. This
comparison measures sensitivity to two written alternatives without inventing their values.

5    Discrepancies and reproduction limits
The largest structural difference from the target experiment is its sampling geometry. The synthetic
coordinates in the original paper come from actual seismicity and station locations, not from a distribu-
tion completely specified in the text. Replacing that geometry changes the null space, ray coverage, and
source–velocity trade-off. A homogeneous initial velocity also changes the nonlinear trajectory relative
to the referenced layered model. We cannot attribute a mismatch with a published error statistic solely
to the inversion algorithm under these changes.
    Our forward grid represents the trough edges more finely than the inversion grid. This is a deliberate
test of model representation error. The 0.3 s station biases are larger than the random pick errors, and
the inverse problem has no explicit station-static parameters. It must accommodate these errors through
source shifts, near-station velocity, or residual misfit. Robust weighting may suppress genuine model
mismatch as if it were an outlier. A lower weighted objective therefore does not by itself establish a
more accurate velocity model; velocity and location errors against the independent truth are reported
separately.
    During development, rejecting an entire trial update when one source left the domain caused the
fixed-model inversion to stall. Projecting individual trial source locations onto the domain corrected that
numerical obstruction. Independent review also found that boundary directions within the ray optimizer
could block tangential motion, and that collapsed ray segments could corrupt endpoint derivatives. Both
cases now have numerical regression checks. A further check found roundoff gradients in coordinate
directions where velocity was exactly constant. Ray optimization and column scaling amplified these into
spurious source-depth sensitivities. Evaluating each interpolation-gradient component with paired nodal
differences preserves this symmetry exactly without changing the mathematical interpolant. Constant-

7.1    A stationary ray can arrive later
An isolated analytic test identifies a remaining branch-selection failure. The invented medium has
v = 4 km/s for −1 ≤ z ≤ 0 km and v = 4 + 0.2z km/s below zero. Both endpoints lie at z = −0.5
km. At horizontal separations of 20 and 40 km, the production tracer returns the straight path in the
constant-velocity layer and declares convergence without an optimizer update. Its projected gradients
are below 3 × 10−16 s/km. Explicit diving rays obtained from Snell’s law arrive 0.0810 and 1.0106 s
earlier, respectively. Fine polygonal evaluations of those diving paths agree with their analytic travel
times within 0.9 microseconds. Thus the discrepancy concerns the selected ray branch, not the stopping
tolerance or a small quadrature error.
    Local stationarity therefore does not establish a first arrival, and a derivative consistent with the
selected direct branch need not describe the first-arrival travel time. The field starting profile also
has a constant above-geoid layer. The current Hayward inversions retain this tracer; the analytic
counterexample does not quantify the error of any particular field ray or attribute the inferred source
displacements to this mechanism. The test definition, numerical results, and source hashes are saved in
data/development/first_arrival_audit/. No observational data enter this diagnostic, and it does
not modify the field calculations.
